7.1: From Fully Connected Layers to Convolutions

To improve my English skills, I’ll be writing my upcoming blog posts entirely in English.

In this Chapter 7 series, I’ll share some fresh insights from Dive into Deep Learning and attempt the exercises—even if my answers might be a bit naive. Although I’ve studied convolutions before in computer vision, the book still offers plenty of remarkable ideas worth noting down.

·Derivation of convolutions

This theoretical derivation of the convolutional operation stems from an extension of the fully connected network. The convolutional form emerges naturally from two guiding principles: translation equivariance and locality.

·Translation equivariance ensures that the network’s response remains consistent regardless of where a feature appears in the input image.

·Locality constrains neurons in the early layers to connect only to nearby, local regions of the input.

Guided by these principles, we begin the derivation. Consider the input X as a vector, where the indices (i,j) denote spatial positions in both the input and output. [H]i, j = Ui, j + Wi, j, k, l[X]k, l Where U is the bias, and we adopt Einstein summation convention. Now, we enforce the principle of translation equivariance: we need to guarantee that H changes only when X changes. Therefore, the weight matrix W and the bias U shouldn’t depend on the coordinate. So we have: [H]i, j = U + Wk, l[X]k, l To make the spatial structure explicit, we re-index the operation around a central position (i, j): [H]i, j = U + ∑a∑bWa, b[X]i + a, j + b Actually, what we are doing is to choose one single point, and then add all the nearby [W·X]. However, take locality into consideration, we should add a bound to our calculation: $$ [H]_{i,j}=U+\sum_{a=-r}^{r}\sum_{b=-r}^{r} W_{a,b}[X]_{i+a,j+b} $$ Here, r defines the receptive field radius, directly relating to the kernel size k(where k=2r+1).

The expression we have arrived at is, in fact, strictly defined as cross-correlation, as the formal mathematical definition of convolution includes a flip of the kernel. However, because the kernel parameters are learned from data during training, this distinction becomes negligible in practice, and the operation is universally termed “convolution” in the context of deep learning.

##　·Exercises

1.Assume that the size of the convolution kernel is r=0. Show that in this case the convolution kernel implements an MLP independently for each set of channels. This leads to the Network in Network architectures.(easy to see)

2.Audio data is often represented as a one-dimensional sequence.

​ 1)When might you want to impose locality and translation invariance for audio?

​ 2)Derive the convolution operations for audio.

​ 3)Can you treat audio using the same tools as computer vision? Hint: use the spectrogram.

3.Why might translation invariance not be a good idea after all? Give an example.

4.Do you think that convolutional layers might also be applicable for text data? Which problems might you encounter with language?

5.What happens with convolutions when an object is at the boundary of an image? (padding)

6.Prove that the convolution is symmetric, i.e., （f， g）=（g， f ）

·Answers

2.1 ·Locality: it is needed because acoustic events occur over short, continuous time intervals(different from the Translation Equivariance in space in computer vision ). A model should integrate information from nearby time samples to recognize such events.(e.g., a phoneme in speech, a note in music, a sound effect)

​ ·Invariance: it is desired because the meaning of a pattern does not change if it occurs a few milliseconds earlier or later.(e.g., the word “hello” is recognizable regardless of its precise starting point in an audio clip.)

2.2 We can simply convert the convolution operation into 1D-time convolution as followed: $$ [H]_{t}=U+\sum_{a=-r}^{r} W_{a}[X]_{t+a} $$ 2.3 Yes, we can directly leverage computer vision tools by converting the 1D time-domain audio signal into a 2D time-frequency representation: the spectrogram.

A spectrogram is obtained by applying the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) to overlapping windows of the audio signal. It has two spatial dimensions:

1.Time (horizontal axis): Corresponds to the original temporal progression.

2.Frequency (vertical axis): Represents the intensity of different frequency components at each time window.

3. Invariance focus on the questions on detecting the item itself. When we are also interested in the location of it, it becomes a terrible idea.(e.g., locating a tumor, detecting specific facial features. )

4. Sure. We could use it via 1D convolutions over word embeddings to detect n-gram patterns. Here are the problems:

  1. Hierarchy & Long Range: Language has nested structure (words→phrases→clauses) and long-distance dependencies. Convolutions’ local, fixed-width view is inefficient at capturing this.

  2. Dynamic Meaning: The same word’s importance changes with context. Static convolution filters cannot adapt weights based on content like attention can.

  3. Information Flow: Distant words must pass through many layers to interact, creating a bottleneck.

6. As defined, the convolution operation is given by: $$ (f*g)=\int_{-\infin}^{\infin}f(y)g(x-y)\mathrm{d}y $$ We aim to prove its commutativity property: $$ \int_{-\infin}^{\infin}f(y)g(x-y)\mathrm{d}y=\int_{-\infin}^{\infin}g(y)f(x-y)\mathrm{d}y $$ Perform the variable substitution t=x−y. It follows that y=x. Substituting into the left-hand side: $$ \int_{-\infin}^{\infin}f(y)g(x-y)\mathrm{d}y=\int_{-\infin}^{\infin}f(x-t)g(t)\mathrm{d}y $$ The last expression is precisely the definition of the right-hand side. Q.E.D
